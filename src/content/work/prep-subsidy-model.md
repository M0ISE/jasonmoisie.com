If PrEP prevents around 99% of HIV infections, and a lifetime of HIV treatment costs the Commonwealth roughly $1 million per person, the economic case for subsidising it looks trivial. So I built a model to ask whether cost is actually the binding constraint.

Working through it, the more interesting finding was about *affordability versus adherence*. We already have genuinely effective tools to prevent transmission. What drives the infection numbers isn't the absence of the tool — it's that people don't know it exists, don't know it applies to them, or don't stay on it. Affordability moves the needle, but without education about what these measures are and who they're for, affordability alone mostly doesn't matter.

That's the useful thing about modelling a wicked problem: it lets the data find the insight rather than your own priors.
